1 neutral default

som

Som is by far the most common Swedish relative clause opener.

Swedish standoutSwedish uses one neutral som across people, animals, things and many abstract antecedents. English splits this between who, which and that; German and Dutch make more form choices.
Shared patternSwedish and English can often omit the relative marker when the relative clause has its own subject. German and Dutch normally keep an overt relative marker.
Register notesom is not informal slang. It is the normal, neutral Swedish choice in both speech and ordinary writing.
Mannen som bor här är läkare.
EnglishThe man who lives here is a doctor.Subject relative: the marker cannot be omitted.
GermanDer Mann, der hier wohnt, ist Arzt.Der agrees and carries nominative case.
DutchDe man die hier woont, is arts.Uses die; the finite verb stays in the relative clause.

If the relative clause introduces its own subject, as in “Boken som jag läste” where jag is the subject of the relative clause, som can be omitted. Otherwise, som cannot normally be omitted.

Boken (som) jag läste var bra.
EnglishThe book (that/which) I read was good.Object relative: that/which can often be omitted.
GermanDas Buch, das ich gelesen habe, war gut.German keeps das; the verb group goes to the end.
DutchHet boek dat ik gelezen heb, was goed.Dutch uses dat with het-words.
2 formal which

vilken / vilket / vilka

Means which – and it must agree with the antecedent in gender and number.

Swedish standoutSwedish has a formal vilken/vilket/vilka option, but everyday Swedish usually avoids it and uses som. This is different from English, where which is not automatically stiff.
Shared patternAll four languages can use a “which-like” strategy in more careful writing, but the normal form differs by language.
Register notevilken / vilket / vilka are mostly written or formal; som is usually more natural in everyday Swedish.
Jag läste en bok, vilken var mycket intressant.
EnglishI read a book, which was very interesting.English which is normal here.
GermanIch las ein Buch, welches sehr interessant war.Welches is possible but formal; das is usually more natural.
DutchIk las een boek dat erg interessant was.Dutch normally uses dat, not a direct equivalent of vilken.
Hon ärvde ett hus, vilket behövde renoveras.
EnglishShe inherited a house, which needed renovating.Which refers to house.
GermanSie erbte ein Haus, das renoviert werden musste.German usually uses das here.
DutchZe erfde een huis dat gerenoveerd moest worden.Het-word antecedent gives dat.
De köpte två stolar, vilka behövde lagas.
EnglishThey bought two chairs, which needed repairing.Which covers plural things too.
GermanSie kauften zwei Stühle, die repariert werden mussten.Plural relative pronoun: die.
DutchZe kochten twee stoelen die gerepareerd moesten worden.Plural/de-word form: die.
3 whole situation

vilket

Vilket can refer back to an entire preceding statement, not just a noun.

Swedish standoutSwedish uses neuter vilket for “the whole previous situation”. This is not the same use as vilket agreeing with an ett-word noun.
Shared patternAll four languages have a way to refer back to an entire previous clause: English which, German was, Dutch wat.
Register noteThis construction is normal in Swedish writing and also usable in speech. A more conversational Swedish paraphrase is often något som ....
Han kom för sent, vilket irriterade oss.
EnglishHe arrived late, which annoyed us.Which refers to the whole fact.
GermanEr kam zu spät, was uns ärgerte.German uses was for the whole situation.
DutchHij kwam te laat, wat ons irriteerde.Dutch uses wat for the whole situation.
Det började regna, vilket förstörde picknicken.
EnglishIt started raining, which ruined the picnic.Which refers to the entire previous event.
GermanEs fing an zu regnen, was das Picknick verdarb.Was refers back to the whole situation.
DutchHet begon te regenen, wat de picknick verpestte.Wat refers to the whole preceding clause.
4 location

där / som + preposition

Där means where. Use it when the antecedent is a place and the relative clause says what happens there.

Swedish standoutSwedish can often avoid a preposition completely with där: huset där jag bor. German commonly needs in dem, and Dutch often uses waarin or waar ... in.
Shared patternAll four languages distinguish a location meaning: “the place where something happens”.
Register noteSwedish där is natural and ordinary. If you use som instead, it does not carry the location meaning by itself: you need a preposition such as i, på or vid. With that preposition in place, som can often be omitted.
Huset där jag bor är gammalt.
EnglishThe house where I live is old.Where marks location.
GermanDas Haus, in dem ich wohne, ist alt.German uses in dem: preposition + relative pronoun.
DutchHet huis waarin ik woon, is oud.Dutch often uses waar + preposition: waarin.
Huset (som) jag bor i är gammalt.
EnglishThe house (that) I live in is old.Swedish needs i to keep the location meaning: huset som jag bor is incomplete. But som itself can be omitted.
GermanDas Haus, in dem ich wohne, ist alt.German normally keeps in dem together.
DutchHet huis waar ik in woon, is oud.Dutch can use a split waar ... in pattern.

Both can work, but they do different grammatical work. Där already means “where”, so no preposition is needed: huset där jag bor. Som is only a general connector here, so the spatial meaning must be rescued by a final preposition: huset (som) jag bor i. The preposition is required; som is optional.

5 direction to

dit / som + preposition

Dit means to which or where to. It marks movement towards a place.

Swedish standoutSwedish has a compact destination word, dit, so it can mark direction without adding till. English often strands to; German and Dutch normally show direction through a prepositional form.
Shared patternAll four languages need to distinguish “where something is” from “where something goes”.
Register notedit is not especially formal. If you use som instead, it does not carry the direction meaning by itself: you need till. With till in place, som can often be omitted.
Staden dit vi reste ligger vid kusten.
EnglishThe town we travelled to is on the coast.English commonly leaves to at the end.
GermanDie Stadt, in die wir reisten, liegt an der Küste.Direction: in + accusative die.
DutchDe stad waarheen we reisden, ligt aan de kust.Waarheen marks direction towards.
Staden (som) vi reste till ligger vid kusten.
EnglishThe town (that) we travelled to is on the coast.Swedish needs till to keep the direction meaning: staden som vi reste is incomplete. But som itself can be omitted.
GermanDie Stadt, in die wir reisten, liegt an der Küste.German expresses direction with in die.
DutchDe stad waar we naartoe reisden, ligt aan de kust.Very natural Dutch: waar ... naartoe.

Both can work, but they do different grammatical work. Dit already means “to where”, so no extra preposition is needed: staden dit vi reste. Som is only a general connector here, so the direction meaning must be rescued by till: staden (som) vi reste till. The preposition is required; som is optional.

6 origin from

varifrån / som + preposition

Varifrån means from which or where from. It is correct and clear, but often more written or formal than (som) ... från.

Swedish standoutSwedish offers two very teachable choices: compact varifrån or neutral (som) ... från. English usually uses a stranded from; German uses aus/von + relative pronoun; Dutch often uses waar ... vandaan.
Shared patternAll four languages mark source or origin separately from ordinary location.
Register notevarifrån is clear and correct but often more written. If you use som instead, it does not carry the source meaning by itself: you need från. With från in place, som can often be omitted.
Landet varifrån de kom ligger långt bort.
EnglishThe country they came from is far away.English often strands from.
GermanDas Land, aus dem sie kamen, liegt weit weg.Source: aus + dative dem.
DutchHet land waar ze vandaan kwamen, ligt ver weg.Everyday Dutch often uses waar ... vandaan.
Landet (som) de kom från ligger långt bort.
EnglishThe country (that) they came from is far away.Swedish needs från to keep the source meaning: landet som de kom is incomplete. But som itself can be omitted.
GermanDas Land, aus dem sie kamen, liegt weit weg.No direct equivalent of Swedish (som) ... från.
DutchHet land waaruit ze kwamen, ligt ver weg.Waaruit is more compact and slightly more written.

Both can work, but they do different grammatical work. Varifrån already means “from where”, so no extra preposition is needed: landet varifrån de kom. Som is only a general connector here, so the source meaning must be rescued by från: landet (som) de kom från. The preposition is required; som is optional.

7 time

när / då / som

När and då both mean when. For learners, när is the safest everyday choice. Då is common in writing and can sound slightly more formal or literary. Som can occur with clear time nouns, but it is a less precise time marker.

Swedish standoutSwedish has two simple time markers, när and då, but the marker can often disappear after clear time nouns: dagen vi träffades. Som is possible here, but it is normally not the best marker for time.
Shared patternAll four languages have a “when” strategy for time relatives, especially in learner-friendly examples.
Register notenär is the everyday learner-safe form. Då is also correct, especially in writing. Som is a weaker time choice, and when it is used in this pattern Swedish usually does not add a preposition.

All four Swedish versions work with the same sentence:

Dagen när vi träffades glömmer jag aldrig. Dagen då vi träffades glömmer jag aldrig. Dagen (som) vi träffades glömmer jag aldrig. Dagen vi träffades glömmer jag aldrig.
EnglishI will never forget the day when we met.When can be omitted: the day we met.
GermanDen Tag, an dem wir uns trafen, vergesse ich nie.German uses an dem for a day/time point.
DutchDe dag waarop we elkaar ontmoetten, vergeet ik nooit.Waarop is common in writing; dat is also common in speech.

For time, när and då mark the temporal relation most clearly. Som is possible when the head noun refers to a clearly delimited time span with a distinct beginning and end, such as dagen, vecka, månad or år. It works less well with vague time expressions such as period, era, epok and similar nouns. In time-related relative clauses, som does not require a preposition.

8 possession

vars

Vars means whose or of which. It does not change form and stands before the noun that belongs to someone or something.

Swedish standoutSwedish vars is unusually simple: it does not change for gender, number or case. German must choose dessen/deren; Dutch often switches between wiens and waarvan.
Shared patternAll four languages have a possessive relative construction corresponding to “whose”.
Register noteEnglish whose is normal for people and possible for organisations. Dutch wiens is quite formal; waarvan is often more natural for organisations and things.
Mannen vars bil står utanför är min granne.
EnglishThe man whose car is parked outside is my neighbour.Whose expresses possession.
GermanDer Mann, dessen Auto draußen steht, ist mein Nachbar.German chooses dessen for masculine/neuter singular antecedents.
DutchDe man wiens auto buiten staat, is mijn buurman.Wiens is correct, but somewhat formal.
Ett företag vars anställda trivs fungerar ofta bättre.
EnglishA company whose employees are happy often works better.Whose can refer to organisations too.
GermanEin Unternehmen, dessen Mitarbeiter zufrieden sind, funktioniert oft besser.Dessen also works for neuter singular Unternehmen.
DutchEen bedrijf waarvan de werknemers tevreden zijn, functioneert vaak beter.Dutch often uses waarvan for organisations/things.

For a general learner, Swedish relative clauses are easiest if the marker is chosen by meaning.

What difference does a relative clause make?

What is the difference between using a relative clause and opting not to? After all, one can say Boken som ligger på bordet är min (The book that is lying on the table is mine), or split the thought in two: Där ligger en bok. Den är min. (There is a book lying there. It is mine.)

While the structural difference might seem minimal at first glance—neither version saving much time or breath—the real distinction is semantic and rhetorical. Slightly simplified, a relative clause performs one of two fascinating functions: it either identifies or characterises.

First, it identifies. It allows the listener or reader to instantly pin down exactly who or what is being discussed. Take the sentence: Mannen som bor här är läkare (The man who lives here is a doctor). Here, the relative clause acts as a pinpoint spotlight, isolating the specific individual without the need for clumsy, iterative phrasing. If we chop it up into Här bor en man och han är läkare (A man lives here, and he is a doctor), that sharp, immediate connection between identity and attributes is diluted.

Second, it characterises. Instead of merely pointing someone out, a relative clause bakes new information directly into their identity. Consider: Jag har en granne vars hund skäller hela natten (I have a neighbour whose dog barks all night). The alternative would be a clunky coordination of facts: Jag har en granne och han har en hund som skäller hela natten (I have a neighbour, and he has a dog that barks all night). In the fragmented version, the connection remains loose: you get a neighbour-fact alongside a dog-fact. But with the relative clause, something much more sophisticated happens. The neighbour is no longer just any neighbour; he is structurally defined by that disruptive dog. The barking is no longer a postscript—it becomes an intrinsic part of how the neighbour is conceptualised. And that is rather elegant.
